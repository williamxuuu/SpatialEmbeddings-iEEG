## (a) Anatomical vs functional vs data-driven vs random/geometric parcellations at matched number of regions: which wins?

### Takeaway
There is no single winner, and the answer depends on the metric. On fit to the data (homogeneity and connectivity fidelity), data-driven and connectivity-derived parcellations win. Purely anatomical atlases (Desikan, Destrieux) do worst, and **they are beaten even by random (Poisson-disk) and geometric (k-means on coordinates) parcellations** at the same parcel count. On downstream prediction (gender, phenotype, decoding), parcel count and softness matter more than parcellation family. The best results come from functional atlases with high dimensionality (around 1000 regions for decoding, around 150-400 nodes for connectome prediction). The choice among reasonable parcellations has "very limited impact" on network analysis. Random and geometric parcellations are established baselines in fMRI. In iEEG, signal-derived electrode embeddings have only recently been compared directly against region labels and MNI coordinates.

### Cited Findings
**Arslan et al. 2018 (NeuroImage), "Human brain mapping: a systematic comparison of parcellation methods for the human cerebral cortex"**
- [VERIFIED] The study compares 10 subject-level and 24 groupwise methods (anatomical, connectivity-driven, random and geometric) on HCP rs-fMRI at multiple resolutions. Methods are evaluated on reproducibility, fidelity to the connectivity data, agreement with task/myelin/cytoarchitecture, and network analysis including gender classification. The random baseline is Poisson-disk sampling (Schirmer 2015), which gives roughly equal-size parcels. The geometric baseline is k-means on spatial vertex coordinates, following Thirion 2014. This is essentially the fMRI analog of a "cubes/grid" control. — [Arslan et al. preprint PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf) (DOI 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.04.014)
- [VERIFIED] On homogeneity and silhouette: "anatomical parcellations Desikan and Destrieux, yield the worst measurements and are surpassed by Geometric and Random. This might suggest that anatomical information alone does not allow to map the brain's functional organisation." Also: "All methods show a performance increasing with the number of parcels." — [same PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf)
- [VERIFIED] On cytoarchitecture and myelin overlap: N-Cuts and Random give the best overlap scores at all resolutions, and anatomical parcellations agree better with myelin and cytoarchitecture than connectivity-driven ones. The authors warn that Dice-type overlap is biased toward uniformly sized and shaped parcels, which favors Geometric, N-Cuts and Random. — [same PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf)
- [VERIFIED] On gender classification from connectomes (SVM, 10-fold CV, 100 subjects): "there is no single winner." Low-resolution parcellations score lower, "perhaps due to the fact that functional information valuable for the discrimination between the two classes fades by averaging the signal in larger parcels." N-Cuts-AVR, Bellec and Arslan do well at several resolutions, and GRASP is best at 50 parcels. Yeo, Shen and Gordon are below average, while Fan and Glasser are good relative to parcellations of similar resolution. — [same PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf)
- [VERIFIED] Conclusion: connectivity-driven parcellations agree much better with rs-fMRI connectivity than anatomical and random parcellations. However, "A general suggestion regarding network analysis would be to use any parcellation available, since this decision seems to have a very limited impact." — [same PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf)

**Dadi et al. 2020 (NeuroImage 221:117126), "Fine-grain atlases of functional modes for fMRI analysis" (DiFuMo)**
- [VERIFIED] The benchmark compares DiFuMo (soft, 64-1024 modes) against BASC (Bellec), Craddock, Gordon (k=333), Schaefer (up to 1000), the Harvard-Oxford anatomical atlas, UKBB ICA, and voxel-level analysis. Tasks cover decoding of 12,334 brain responses across 6 task-fMRI studies, connectome prediction of 7 phenotype targets in about 2,500 subjects, GLM, compression, and meta-analysis. — [arXiv 2003.05405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05405)
- [VERIFIED] Decoding: "the higher the atlas resolution, the better the predictions. Using DiFuMo k = 1024 or Schaefer k = 1000 gives the best performance and, on average, outperforms voxel-level prediction." The authors attribute this to "data reduction acts here as a welcome regularization." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.05405)
- [VERIFIED] Connectome prediction behaves differently: "high-resolution atlases do not provide the best performance, likely because the complexity of the statistical models increases with the square of the number of nodes." The best atlases are DiFuMo k=256, then Craddock k=400, then BASC k=444. **The optimal dimensionality depends on the target, consistently across atlases: about 150 for age, about 300 for ASD, PTSD and IQ, and about 50 for Alzheimer's disease and drug use.** — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.05405)
- [VERIFIED] Ranking of factors: "biggest gains in analysis come from increasing the dimensionality of brain sub-divisions [...] the second most important aspect of a parcellation appears that it be soft." — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.05405)

**Thirion et al. 2014 (Front. Neurosci. 8:167), "Which fMRI clustering gives good brain parcellations?"**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] The study compares Ward, spectral and k-means clustering, plus geometric parcellations, on simulated data and two task-fMRI datasets, scoring goodness-of-fit and bootstrap reproducibility. Ward performs best on both reproducibility and accuracy. Reproducibility favors coarser solutions, so the choice of k is an accuracy/stability trade-off. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2014.00167/full); [PMC4076743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4076743)
- [IMPRESSION] Thirion 2014 also reports that geometric parcellations (k-means on coordinates) are a reasonable baseline at high k, and that BIC always favors more parcels. Arslan 2018, which I verified, cites Thirion 2014 for the BIC bias. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2014.00167/full)

**Gordon et al. 2016 (Cereb Cortex 26:288-303) and Schaefer et al. 2018 (Cereb Cortex 28:3095)**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Gordon built a boundary-mapping parcellation of 333 parcels. Each parcellation was compared against a null model of the same parcels randomly rotated on the sphere, and "no other parcellation was as homogenous as or had as large a difference compared with its null model." This rotated-parcel null is the standard "same parcel sizes, randomized placement" control. It is conceptually close to a shuffled-label control. — [PMC4677978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4677978)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Schaefer's gwMRF parcellations (100-1000 parcels) were more homogeneous than four previously published parcellations across several datasets. — [PubMed 28981612](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28981612/); [PDF](https://people.csail.mit.edu/ythomas/publications/2018LocalGlobal-CerebCor.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] An independent re-evaluation against 1000 rotated nulls found z-scores of about 1.76 for Schaefer400, 0.02 for Glasser and -1.75 for Gordon (dilated version, different data). Rankings are therefore not stable across datasets. — [BDEC arXiv 2309.09984](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09984)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] A boundary-based evaluation paper ran Monte-Carlo random-parcellation tests and reported that **common anatomical parcellations do not perform better than chance**, whereas rs-fMRI-based parcellations perform well. — [PMC6519695](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519695)

**Reviews and other comparisons**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Eickhoff, Yeo & Genon 2018, Nat Rev Neurosci 19:672-686, "Imaging-based parcellations of the human brain", reviews local-property (cytoarchitecture, gradient) and global-property (connectivity, clustering) strategies. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41583-018-0071-7)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Pervaiz et al. 2020, NeuroImage 211:116604, evaluated more than 9000 pipeline variants (parcellation, FC estimator, tangent space, predictor) on about 14,000 UKB and HCP subjects. I did not read which parcellation won. My [IMPRESSION] is that data-driven ICA/PROFUMO-type parcellations did well and that parcellation choice mattered less than tangent-space and shrinkage choices. Verify before citing. — [PMC7086233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7086233)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Behavior prediction requires 40-60 gradients to match parcellation-based FC. — [PMC10192836](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10192836/)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Salehi et al. found that functional parcel definitions change with task state, and that parcel size alone can predict task condition. — [PubMed 31740342](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31740342/)
- [IMPRESSION] Abraham et al. 2017 (NeuroImage, ABIDE autism classification) found that atlases learned from the data itself (MSDL / dictionary learning / ICA) beat reference atlases (Harvard-Oxford, AAL, Yeo) for connectome classification. Not verified this session. — search "Deriving reproducible biomarkers from multi-site resting-state data Abraham 2017"
- [IMPRESSION] Craddock et al. 2012 (HBM) used random parcellations as a baseline for spatially-constrained spectral clustering. At about 200+ parcels, random parcellations were roughly as homogeneous as clustered ones. Not verified. — search "Craddock 2012 whole brain fMRI atlas spatially constrained spectral clustering"

**iEEG analogs: grouping or embedding electrodes by signal similarity**
- [VERIFIED abstract] Javadzadeh et al., NeurIPS 2026 workshop (Symmetry & Geometry in Neural Representations), "Learning Data-Driven Shared Coordinates for Cross-Subject Intracranial Recordings". They trained a contrastive CNN on 10-s LFP segments from 20 subjects, producing a 32-d learned coordinate per channel. Region labels were used only to define positive and negative pairs. Held-out-channel k-NN accuracy was 52.84%. In a held-out-entire-region test, otherwise identical pooled transformers were conditioned on **region labels vs MNI locations vs learned coordinates**. The learned coordinates significantly improved cross-region waveform prediction in both hemispheres. **This is the closest existing comparison to this project's setup.** It does not mention cubes/grids or shuffled-label controls. — [NeurIPS listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/157293)
- [VERIFIED abstract] Javadzadeh et al., arXiv 2510.27090, used Siamese contrastive functional embeddings per electrode with a transformer across channels on a 20-subject basal-ganglia-thalamic SEEG dataset. The abstract argues MNI coordinates "often fail to capture true functional similarity" but gives no numbers. — [arXiv 2510.27090](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27090)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] seegnificant (Mentzelopoulos et al. 2024, arXiv 2411.10458) is a multi-subject sEEG transformer (21 subjects) with an MNI-derived positional encoding. **Ablating the MNI positional encoding gave no significant performance loss.** This is direct prior evidence that coordinate encodings may add little for a behavioral decoding target. — [arXiv 2411.10458](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.10458v1)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] A 2026 arXiv paper benchmarks positional-encoding strategies for transformer EEG (scalp) foundation models. Not read. — [arXiv 2605.29754](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29754)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Saboo et al. 2019, Sci Rep: unsupervised GMM clustering of spectral-power and synchrony features from 115 patients (verbal memory task) found distinct sets of task-active electrodes across subjects. — [Sci Rep](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-019-53925-5)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Banks et al. 2023, PLOS Biol: resting-state iEEG from 49 participants, embedding recording sites in a low-dimensional space where distance reflects functional-connectivity similarity. — [PLOS Biol](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002239)

### Inferences
- The fMRI literature predicts that, at matched K, **a regular grid or random parcellation should not lose badly to an anatomical atlas** (Desikan-like), and may beat it on fit-to-signal metrics. A cubes-vs-atlas comparison at matched K therefore needs a clear hypothesis. "Atlas beats cubes" is not the default expectation.
- Parcel count K is the dominant factor in fMRI (Dadi). Any atlas-vs-cube-vs-shuffled comparison must match K and, ideally, the parcel-size distribution, as Arslan's Dice-bias caveat shows. Otherwise differences will reflect granularity, not semantics.
- The shuffled-label control (atlas labels permuted across regions, keeping the partition) is analogous to, but different from, the fMRI rotated-parcel null. The rotated null keeps parcel shapes and moves them spatially. Label shuffling keeps the partition but breaks cross-subject correspondence. I found no iEEG paper using region-label shuffling as a control for a spatial embedding, so it looks novel in the iEEG transformer context (not exhaustively verified).
- The Javadzadeh NeurIPS 2026 workshop paper and the seegnificant MNI ablation are the two prior results most directly relevant to novelty claims. The former already shows signal-derived coordinates beating region labels and MNI. The latter shows MNI encodings can be ablated without loss.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain numeric effect sizes for Pervaiz 2020, Abraham 2017 or Craddock 2012 (not read).
- Bellec BASC original evaluations were not retrieved.
- MEG/source-space parcellation comparisons (e.g. leakage-limited atlases) were not searched.
- I found no iEEG study that explicitly compares atlas parcels vs regular cubes vs random partitions at matched K.

## (b) Soft or probabilistic assignment of electrodes or voxels to regions

### Takeaway
iEEG localization tools commonly output a hard nearest-label. Some also give partial-volume or probability information: Qin 2017 gives per-contact atlas probabilities, iELVis gives a gray/white "proximal tissue density", and GMPI gives distance to the gray/white boundary. Localization error from brain shift can reach 10-20 mm for subdural grids, so a contact can span or straddle regions. In fMRI, the only clear evidence that soft beats hard at matched dimensionality is Dadi 2020 (DiFuMo). I found no iEEG decoding or ML paper that tests soft region membership against hard labels. A Gaussian-weighted region-mix encoding appears untested in the literature I found.

### Cited Findings
**Probabilistic and partial-volume labeling tools for iEEG**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Qin et al. 2017, Front Neuroinform 11:10, "Automatic and Precise Localization and Cortical Labeling of Subdural and Depth Intracranial Electrodes". It localizes SEEG and ECoG contacts from pre-op T1 and post-op CT, and **gives each contact a probability of belonging to each brain area or functional network** (Brodmann and fMRI-network atlases). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fninf.2017.00010); [PMC5314105](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5314105/). PMC full text was blocked by a CAPTCHA, so the method details (sphere radius, voxel counting) are unverified. My [IMPRESSION] is that it counts atlas voxels within a small neighborhood around each contact.
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] iELVis (Groppe et al. 2017, J Neurosci Methods; bioRxiv 069179) was the first public tool to correct brain shift, map electrodes to an average brain, and support overlays. v1.1.0 added the **proximal tissue density (PTD) index**, which counts gray and white matter 1x1x1 mm voxels around each depth contact. It was reported for 3158 depth contacts in 23 subjects (Mercier et al. 2017-2019 J Neural Eng figure). — [MATLAB File Exchange](https://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/57317-ielvis); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2016/11/30/069179.full.pdf); [PTD figure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5075506/figure/F9)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] The gray matter proximity index (GMPI) scores each contact by its distance to the white/gray boundary, and the open-source GMPIE pipeline computes it automatically. — [PMC6043781](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6043781)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Gray vs white classification of SEEG contacts can be done from the recorded signal plus relative depth alone. — [PMC7815703](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7815703/)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Brainstorm sEEG-Suite does semi-automated contact localization with atlas labeling. Semi-automated atlas labels agreed with an alternative labeling method for **only 82% of more than 17,000 contacts**. This indicates substantial label ambiguity between methods. — [PMC12458114](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12458114/)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] LocateElectrodes labels each contact by atlas region, tissue class (GM/WM/CSF) and resection mask. — [Front Neuroinform 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fninf.2018.00040)

**Localization error magnitude**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Brain-surface deformation of 10-20 mm can occur around electrodes or in deeper structures, which precludes accurate localization from post-implant CT alone (Fudan/Huashan, 15 patients). Depth electrodes shift less than subdural grids and strips. — [PMC10197594](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10197594/); [bioRxiv 2023.05.08.539503](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.05.08.539503.full.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] iELVis brain-shift correction is described as achieving millimeter-scale accuracy. — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2016/11/30/069179.full.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Mercier et al. 2022, NeuroImage, "Advances in human intracranial electroencephalography research, guidelines and good practices", has a dedicated section on electrode localization. I did not extract its numeric statements. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/faffe7d2c35945f1a5c18f14c2fac722); [PubMed 38224783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38224783/)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Formal Bayesian uncertainty quantification of contact position exists for DBS leads at sub-voxel level (Athawale et al.), but not for iEEG. — [Utah PDF](https://www.sci.utah.edu/publications/Ath2018a/athawale2019.pdf)
- [IMPRESSION] Hermes et al. 2010 (J Neurosci Methods) reported median ECoG projection errors of a few mm after correction. Additional error comes from MNI normalization (several mm of inter-subject anatomical variability), and SEEG contacts are 2 mm long with 3.5 mm spacing (Dixi/Ad-Tech). Not verified.

**Soft vs hard in decoding and ML**
- [VERIFIED] Dadi 2020: "For a given dimensionality, soft modes tend to outperform hard parcellations, whether they are derived with ICA or dictionary learning." DiFuMo k=1024 captures 70% of voxel-level signal variance. Soft modes "better capture gradients" than discrete parcellations such as BASC. The softness advantage is ranked second to dimensionality. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.05405)
- [VERIFIED abstract] The Javadzadeh NeurIPS 2026 paper uses region labels only as soft contrastive supervision, so its per-channel coordinate is continuous rather than a hard label. That is soft in a different sense: learned and signal-derived, not geometric. — [NeurIPS listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/157293)

### Inferences
- A Gaussian-weighted mix of region identities around each contact is a natural generalization of Qin-style neighborhood probabilities and PTD. I found no iEEG paper that feeds such soft membership into a decoder and compares it against hard labels, so the experiment looks novel. The likely closest precedent in spirit is DiFuMo (soft > hard at matched K) in fMRI.
- Prior work supports choosing a Gaussian width on the scale of the localization error. A sigma of about 2-5 mm is plausible for depth contacts. For subdural contacts, or after MNI normalization, a larger sigma could be justified given shift up to 10-20 mm. The 82% inter-method label agreement suggests that around 18% of hard labels are ambiguous. That fraction sets an upper bound on how much soft labeling could help via label-noise reduction.
- Expected effect size: Dadi ranks softness second to dimensionality, so a soft-vs-hard difference will likely be smaller than a K-change effect.

### Gaps
- I did not verify Qin 2017's exact probabilistic method or numbers (CAPTCHA).
- I did not verify MNE-Python or Brainstorm options for probabilistic or nearest-k labels. My [IMPRESSION] is that MNE's `mne.get_montage_volume_labels` returns all atlas labels within a distance and their proportions, which is effectively soft labeling. Verify at https://mne.tools.
- "ELAB" was not found. I found no reliable source for a tool by that name.
- I found no study showing that probabilistic or soft atlases outperform hard labels for iEEG decoding.

## (c) Does the best spatial granularity depend on how localized the decoded signal or task is?

### Takeaway
Yes, partly. In fMRI connectome prediction the optimal parcel count differs by target, consistently across atlases: about 50 for AD and drug use, about 150 for age, about 300 for IQ, ASD and PTSD (Dadi 2020). Fine-grain atlases are best for task decoding. The "spatial scale of information" debate (Op de Beeck, Kamitani, Swisher, Kriegeskorte) shows that decodable information exists at multiple scales. Smoothing often does not destroy decoding, which complicates simple locality arguments. In iEEG, high-gamma activity is decorrelated at 4 mm and denser grids improve speech and motor decoding. Sensory/motor targets therefore plausibly favor fine resolution, but no iEEG study directly maps optimal parcel granularity against task locality.

### Cited Findings
**fMRI: target-dependent optimal granularity**
- [VERIFIED] Dadi 2020: "Different outcomes have different optimal dimensionality, consistently across atlases (Figure A5): k ∼ 150 for age prediction; k ∼ 300 for Autism Spectrum Disorder, PTSD, or IQ prediction; and k ∼ 50 for Alzheimer's Disease and drug use prediction." For task decoding, performance increases monotonically up to k≈1000. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.05405)
- [VERIFIED] Arslan 2018: at coarse resolution, information valuable for the discrimination "fades by averaging the signal in larger parcels." All methods gain homogeneity with more parcels. — [PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf)

**fMRI: spatial scale of decodable information**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Op de Beeck 2010, NeuroImage, "Spatial smoothing hurts localization but not information": orientation classification survived 8 mm FWHM smoothing. Brants et al. 2011 used smoothing manipulation to infer spatial scale, comparing category vs exemplar selectivity in ventral visual cortex. Hendriks et al. 2017 reviewed smoothing effects on MVPA, including motor and auditory cortex. — [Hendriks et al. 2017 Front Neurol](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2017.00222/full)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Kamitani & Sawahata 2010 countered that smoothing need not hurt an optimal classifier, so smoothing robustness does not by itself demonstrate coarse-scale organization. — [Kamitani 2010 PDF](https://www.cfn.upenn.edu/stslab/wiki/lib/exe/fetch.php/fmri_club:preprocess1:kamitani_2010_neuroimage.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] In 1 mm human data, most orientation information lay at millimeter scales, with small contributions from global biases above about 1 cm. A separate study showed that coarse neural effects plus voxel gain differences can produce spurious high-spatial-frequency pattern information. — [PMC3740242](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3740242); [Sci Rep 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-07036-8.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Watson, Young & Andrews 2016 (NeuroImage) found multiple scales of organization for object selectivity in ventral visual cortex. — [PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/95789/1/WatsonYoungAndrews_NIMG_16Nov2015.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] Kriegeskorte, Goebel & Bandettini 2006 (PNAS), searchlight: in simulation on 2 mm isotropic voxels, a 4 mm radius gave the best detection, and detection "did not require a close match between the size of the searchlight and the informative area." A 2012 note shows the number of informative searchlights grows with radius, which affects interpretation. — [PMC1383651](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1383651); [nilearn docs](https://nilearn.github.io/0.7.1/decoding/searchlight.html); [arXiv 1210.6317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1210.6317)

**iEEG: focality of high-gamma and electrode density**
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] In high-density human speech-cortex ECoG, the envelope and phase of gamma and high-gamma (30-150 Hz) were largely uncorrelated (<90%) at 4 mm, the smallest spacing tested. Correlation fell off with distance, and spatial resolution depends on frequency band, with lower frequencies more spatially correlated (Muller et al. 2016, "Spatial resolution dependence on spectral frequency in human speech cortex electrocorticography"). — [PubMed 27578414](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27578414/); [PMC5081035](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5081035)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] High-density µECoG improved speech decoding by 35% over standard intracranial recordings, and accurate decoding depended on high spatial resolution (Duraivel et al. 2023, Nat Commun). — [Nat Commun](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-42555-1.pdf)
- [SEARCH-SUMMARY] In thin-film µECoG with 2 mm spacing, decoding accuracy improved with electrode density in all 3 patients. — [PMC5789448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789448)

### Inferences
- The prediction for this project is that tasks with focal, sensory or motor high-gamma correlates (auditory, speech, movement) should benefit from fine spatial encodings (fine cubes, coordinates, small-sigma soft mixes). Distributed or state-like targets (arousal, memory success, sleep stage, cross-subject phenotype) should saturate at coarse K. This mirrors Dadi's target-dependent optimum. It is a hypothesis built from fMRI and ECoG density results, not a demonstrated iEEG result.
- In sEEG, sampling is sparse: typically a few hundred contacts over about 10-15 shafts. The effective spatial resolution of a region encoding is therefore limited by coverage, not by atlas granularity. Fine parcels will contain few electrodes per subject and few shared across subjects, so the overfitting argument (Dadi: connectome complexity grows with K²) predicts an interior optimum.
- Smoothing-robustness results (Op de Beeck) warn that coarse encodings may perform nearly as well even for "focal" tasks. A null difference between granularities would not prove that the signal is non-local.

### Gaps
- I found no iEEG or sEEG study that systematically varies the granularity of the spatial encoding (atlas level or cube size) and relates the optimum to task locality. This appears to be an open, novel experiment.
- I did not retrieve searchlight-radius-by-task studies (e.g., optimal radius for auditory vs frontal decoding).
- MEG spatial-scale and decoding work was not covered.
