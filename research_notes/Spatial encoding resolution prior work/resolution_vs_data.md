# Optimal encoding resolution vs. amount of data: prior evidence outside iEEG foundation models

Scope note: I ran about 15 search and fetch calls. Each item is marked **[verified]** if I read the primary text or abstract (or a search excerpt from it), or **[impression]** if it comes from background knowledge or a secondary summary I did not check against the paper. Search date: 2026-11-20.

## Q1. fMRI and connectomics: does the optimal parcellation granularity depend on sample size, number of subjects or scan length?

### Takeaway
The fMRI literature shows that an optimal parcel count exists, that it depends on the task and the selection criterion, and (in one paper) that choosing the right granularity matters most when samples are small. I found **no study that systematically shows the optimal number of parcels moving to finer values as N (subjects or scan time) grows**. One structural-MRI paper names this as an open question. The claim "the optimum shifts finer with more data" looks testable and largely unclaimed in neuroimaging.

### Cited Findings
- **Dohmatob, Richard, Pinho & Thirion (2021), "Brain topography beyond parcellations: Local gradients of functional maps", NeuroImage 229:117706.** It predicts task maps in left-out subjects with parcel-wise linear models, aggregated over many random parcellations. Abstract excerpt: "Our experiments demonstrate the existence of an optimal cardinality of the parcellation to capture local gradients of functional maps. **The effect is pronounced in the small-sample regime and it diminishes as the sample size increases.**" Highlights: "Using about 200 parcels yields highest accuracy for local linear rest-to-task map prediction". Combining several parcellations also improves accuracy. [verified: abstract and highlights via search excerpts; I could not open the full text because DOAJ returned 403] — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348638654_Brain_topography_beyond_parcellations_Local_gradients_of_functional_maps); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/13b8cc9936384090a3b46291ad4bedbd); [dblp](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/journals/neuroimage/DohmatobRPT21.html)
  - Caveat: the excerpt says the *size of the effect* of choosing the optimal cardinality shrinks as n grows. It does **not** say the optimum moves finer. The full text needs checking to see whether the optimum location shifts with n.
- **Thirion, Varoquaux, Dohmatob & Poline (2014), "Which fMRI clustering gives good brain parcellations?", Front. Neurosci. 8:167.** Accuracy criteria (BIC, cross-validated likelihood) put the optimum at a very large number of parcels: "3000 ≤ K ≤ 7000" on the Localizer dataset (128 subjects), and "K* ≤ 3000" on HCP (67 subjects). Reproducibility "displays a clear optimum value at K ≃ 200", and the authors recommend "a conservative choice K ~ 200–500". In simulations, BIC over-selects and reproducibility is "over-conservative". The paper does **not** vary the number of subjects or the amount of data systematically. It only notes that HCP had fewer subjects and lower SNR, giving larger bootstrap variability and "weak changes of the optimal values". The authors say the choice of K "remains open and should be addressed in a data-driven fashion". [verified, PMC full text] — [PMC4076743](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4076743/)
  - Useful for framing: the optimal resolution depends on the criterion. Fit or accuracy favors fine resolution, while stability or variance favors coarse resolution. This mirrors the bias-variance split.
- **Dadi et al. (2020), "Fine-grain atlases of functional modes for fMRI analysis" (DiFuMo), NeuroImage 221.** DiFuMo atlases range from 64 to 1024 modes and were trained on 2.4 TB of data from 27 studies. Benchmarks include decoding from 12,334 brain responses and connectome biomarkers for 2,500 individuals. "Analyses on high-dimensional modes achieve similar statistical performance as at the voxel level." [verified, arXiv abstract] — [arXiv 2003.05405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05405)
  - Per-target optimal dimensionality (search-result excerpt of the paper; I did not read the figure): best overall was DiFuMo k=256, then Craddock k=400 and BASC k=444. Optima differ by target: about 150 for age, about 300 for ASD, PTSD and IQ, and about 50 for Alzheimer's disease and drug use. Higher dimensionality "raises significant computational and statistical challenges". [verified as excerpt only] — [ADNI-hosted PDF](https://adni.loni.usc.edu/adni-publications/Dadi_2020_Neuroimage.pdf)
  - Inference to check: the targets with coarse optima (AD, drug use) are probably also the smaller cohorts. That would be indirect evidence for the hypothesis, but the paper does not make this analysis and the confound with target type is strong.
- **Dadi et al. (2019), "Benchmarking functional connectome-based predictive models for resting-state fMRI", NeuroImage 192.** This study ran 6 cohorts, about 2,000 individuals and more than 240 pipelines. Functionally defined regions (dictionary learning or ICA) predicted best, with tangent-space connectomes and non-sparse linear classifiers. The search did not show an analysis of dimensionality against n. [verified, abstract-level] — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01824205)
- **Abraham et al. (2017), NeuroImage 147:736–745 (ABIDE, 871 subjects after QC, 17 sites).** "Brain areas extracted from large R-fMRI datasets outperform reference atlases". I did not retrieve the region-count results. [verified, abstract-level] — [arXiv 1611.06066](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1611.06066)
- **Pervaiz, Vidaurre, Woolrich & Smith (2020), "Optimising network modelling methods for fMRI", NeuroImage 211:116604.** The study used UKB (13,301 subjects, 6-min scans) and HCP (1,003 subjects, 60-min scans). sICA was run at d=15/50/200 on HCP and d=25/100 on UKB (21/55 after artefact removal). Other parcellations: Shen 268, Yeo 100/200/400, and PROFUMO 50. General finding: "increasing dimensionality leads to an increase in predictive power." Dimensionality was **not** analysed against subject count or scan length. The scan-length subsampling (HCP cut to 15 and 5 min) addressed only shrinkage and tangent space. [verified, first ~55% of PMC full text] — [PMC7086233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7086233/)
  - Opportunity: the two datasets differ about 13x in N and 10x in scan length, but the tested d grids differ too, so no direct comparison is possible.
- **ABCD structural MRI parcellation scaling (Cerebral Cortex 2022, "Performance scaling for structural MRI surface parcellations: a machine learning analysis in the ABCD Study").** Per the search excerpt, the paper states that "the influence of sample size on the observed parcellation performance scaling remains unknown." This explicitly names the question as open. [verified as search excerpt only] — [doi:10.1093/cercor/bhac060](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhac060)
- **A connectome study cited in the search** reports that finer parcellations raise prediction accuracy but make feature weights less reliable. I did not identify the exact paper (search result only). [impression] — see [Arslan et al. 2018 parcellation survey PDF](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~sa1013/pub/2017-neuroimage-brain-parcellation-survey.pdf) and [review PMC9291407](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9291407/), which states that it is not known a priori which granularity describes the data better.
- **"Scaling laws for group-constrained, subject-specific task fMRI analyses"** (bioRxiv, 2026). This appeared in search results and is possibly relevant, but I did not read it. [unread] — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.22.740076v1.full)

### Inferences
- The closest neuroimaging precedent is Dohmatob et al. 2021. Combined with Thirion 2014's split between the accuracy optimum (fine) and the reproducibility optimum (coarse), it supports the bias-variance framing. However, neither paper plots the optimal K against N. A clean "optimum shifts finer with more subjects" curve appears to be novel in neuroimaging, as far as this search found.
- Thirion 2014 is the cleanest citation for "finer is better for fit, coarser is better for stability".

### Gaps
- I could not read the Dohmatob 2021 full text, so I cannot confirm whether the optimum location moves with n or only the benefit of choosing it.
- I did not verify the Schaefer 2018 resolution analyses, Kong et al. (individual-specific parcellations) or Luppi et al. for sample-size interactions.
- The DiFuMo per-target optima come from an excerpt only. Cohort sizes per target were not checked.

## Q2. Multi-subject spiking population models (POYO, POYO+, NDT2, NDT3, unit embeddings): do they study embedding granularity against data?

### Takeaway
I found no study of identity or location embedding *granularity* against data size in these models. They report data-scaling of the whole model, and some report saturation that they attribute to tokenization or sensor variability.

### Cited Findings
- **NDT2 (Ye, Collinger, Wehbe & Gaunt, NeurIPS 2023)** pretrains across sessions, subjects and tasks with context embeddings and allows rapid adaptation to new contexts. [verified, abstract] — [NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/fe51de4e7baf52e743b679e3bdba7905-Abstract.html)
- A follow-up paper ("universal translator", arXiv 2407.14668) reports that NDT2 multi-session pretraining "benefits from scaling from 1 to 10 sessions, but we only observe marginal gains or no improvement going from 10 to 34 sessions". It attributes this to the fixed patch size and to neuron-count variation. [verified, search excerpt] — [arXiv 2407.14668](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.14668)
- The NDT3 paper argues that scaling "seems unlikely to resolve limitations stemming from sensor variability and output stereotypy". [verified, search excerpt] — [arXiv 2504.08201](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.08201.pdf)
- **[impression]** POYO (Azabou et al., NeurIPS 2023) and POYO+ give each unit and session a learned embedding. New sessions are handled by learning only those embeddings ("unit identification"). As far as I know, they show model and data scaling but do not compare coarse (region-level) and fine (unit-level) identity embeddings as a function of data. Not verified in this pass.

### Inferences
- Spike-model work uses the finest possible identity (one embedding per unit). It offers no evidence about a granularity-vs-N optimum, so the iEEG experiment would not be duplicating it.

### Gaps
- I did not read the POYO, POYO+ or NDT3 full texts. Their ablations should be checked for region-level embeddings.

## Q3. Machine learning: positional-encoding bandwidth, hash grids, categorical cardinality, hierarchical smoothing, spatial grain vs. data

### Takeaway
Across ML and statistics, the best "resolution" consistently trades bias against variance. Fourier-feature bandwidth has an underfit-to-overfit optimum. Target encoding and partial pooling shrink sparse categories toward coarser means, with weights set by count. In species distribution models, larger samples make models more sensitive to grain. However, explicit "optimal resolution vs. N" curves are rare outside classical nonparametrics. One recommender scaling study found that the optimal embedding *dimension* **shrinks** with data scale, which is a caution against assuming monotonicity for every axis of capacity.

### Cited Findings
- **Tancik et al. (2020), Fourier features (NeurIPS).** Sweeping the Gaussian scale σ moves "from underfitting (low scale, recovered function too low frequency) to overfitting (high scale, recovered function too high frequency), with the best generalization performance in the middle". Distribution shape matters less than scale, and σ=10 was best for their natural images. The tuning is done per task, **not** as a function of the number of training samples. [verified, project page and search excerpt] — [project page](https://bmild.github.io/fourfeat); [arXiv 2006.10739](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.10739)
- **Wang, Wang & Perdikaris (2021)** argue that σ should be chosen so that the leading NTK eigenvector frequencies match the target's frequencies. [verified, excerpt] — [arXiv 2012.10047](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.10047)
- **[impression]** Inference from NTK theory and spectral bias (Rahaman et al. 2019): with fixed training-point density, a bandwidth above the sampling density (roughly Nyquist) leads to aliasing or overfitting. The usable bandwidth therefore grows with sample density. This is the continuous-coordinate analogue of the hypothesis. I found no paper that plots optimal σ against N.
- **[impression]** Multi-resolution hash grids (Müller et al., Instant-NGP, 2022) avoid choosing one resolution by stacking levels from coarse to fine. This is an architectural hedge comparable to aggregating multiple parcellations (Dohmatob 2021). I did not find a scaling study.
- **Micci-Barreca (2001), SIGKDD Explorations 3(1):27–32.** This is the empirical-Bayes target encoding for high-cardinality categoricals. Per-level means are shrunk toward the global mean, with a weight that depends on the level's count. Pargent et al. (2022) describe the smoothing as shrinking effects "towards the global mean". [verified, citation and secondary description] — [Pargent et al., Comput. Stat. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00180-022-01207-6); [benchmark README](https://github.com/jorisvandenbossche/target-encoder-benchmarks/blob/master/README.md)
  - This is the formal per-category answer to "fine regions have few electrodes": the effective resolution adapts to count. The hierarchical version (category to parent category to global) is the multilevel or partial-pooling model of Gelman & Hill [impression].
- **Recommender scaling (Ardalani et al. 2022, "Understanding Scaling Laws for Recommendation Models").** Per the search excerpt, the best embedding dimension **shrinks** as the data scaling factor grows (for example, 256 was best at lower factors). Performance depends strongly on dataset size and weakly on model size. [verified as search excerpt only; check the figure] — [arXiv 2208.08489](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.08489)
  - Caution: this concerns embedding *width*, not partition granularity. It shows that intuitions such as "more data allows more capacity per token" can fail.
- **Species distribution models (ecology), grain against sample size.** Guisan et al. (2007), "Sensitivity of predictive species distribution models to change in grain size" (50 species, 10-fold coarsening): degradation was modest overall, and **larger training samples gave better models that were also more sensitive to grain change**. That is, the cost of coarse grain grows with data, which is consistent with the optimum shifting finer as N rises. [verified, abstract-level excerpts] — [ANU record](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/sensitivity-of-predictive-species-distribution-models-to-change-i/); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/sensitivity-of-predictive-species-distribution-models-to-change-i/)
- A review notes that sample size, positional accuracy, grid resolution, algorithm and species specialisation can interact, but prior work mostly tested them additively. [verified, excerpt] — [PMC5683637](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5683637)
- "Species distribution model transferability and model grain size – finer may not always be better": a finer grain does not always transfer better to new regions. This is a useful analogue for *new subjects*. [verified, title] — [PMC5940916](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5940916/)
- Giant panda SDM: a coarser grain reduced accuracy at small extents, and larger extents offset it. Few studies examine grain-extent interactions. [verified, excerpt] — [PMC6787011](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6787011/)
- **[impression]** Uber's H3 resolution choice, and spatial binning in geostatistics (the modifiable areal unit problem), are chosen by rule of thumb. I found no data-scaling theory for them.
- **[impression]** Bayesian nonparametrics: under a Dirichlet-process (CRP) prior, the expected number of clusters grows as α·log n. This is a canonical "model granularity grows with data" result and could be cited as a formal analogue.

### Inferences
- The SDM result (larger N makes models more sensitive to grain) is the closest empirical analogue outside neuroscience for a granularity × N interaction in a *spatial* setting.
- Target encoding and partial pooling suggest a design alternative: hierarchical embeddings (lobe + parcel + coordinate, with shrinkage or dropout toward the parent). These should beat any single fixed resolution and smooth out the optimum curve. This is worth including as an arm of the experiment.

### Gaps
- I found no paper giving optimal Fourier σ or hash-grid resolution as a function of training-set size.
- The recommender-scaling claim needs checking against its figure.
- I did not find work on learned-embedding cardinality (for example, the number of hash buckets) against data size; searches were not exhaustive.

## Q4. Is there a canonical theory to cite as the formal basis, and what is its analogue for embeddings?

### Takeaway
Yes. Classical histogram and partitioning-estimate theory gives exactly the predicted law. The optimal bin width shrinks as n^(-1/3) in 1-D (Scott 1979; Freedman & Diaconis 1981). For partition-based (regressogram) regression in d dimensions under Lipschitz smoothness, the optimal cell side is h ∝ n^(-1/(d+2)), from balancing variance ~1/(n·h^d) against squared bias ~h². In 3-D brain space (d=3), the optimal parcel side should therefore shrink like n^(-1/5), and the optimal number of parcels should grow like n^(3/5).

### Cited Findings
- **Scott (1979), "On optimal and data-based histograms", Biometrika 66(3):605–610.** Minimizing MISE gives h* = 3.49·s·n^(-1/3) for Gaussian data. [verified, secondary sources] — [Wikipedia: Scott's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott%27s_rule); [Knuth, arXiv physics/0605197](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0605197)
- **Freedman & Diaconis (1981), "On the histogram as a density estimator: L2 theory", Z. Wahrscheinlichkeitstheorie / PTRF 57(4):453–476.** This gives h = 2·IQR·n^(-1/3). The result requires a finite integral of the squared density derivative, so it does not hold for the uniform density, for example. [verified, secondary] — [Wikipedia: Freedman–Diaconis rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedman%E2%80%93Diaconis_rule)
- Scott and Freedman–Diaconis "fully characterized the asymptotic effect of bin width on MISE", and the optimal width shrinks at n^(-1/3). [verified] — [Wand (1997), "Data-based choice of histogram bin width", Am. Stat.](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~rnugent/PCMI2016/papers/WandBinWidth.pdf)
- **Partitioning regression estimates (Györfi, Kohler, Krzyżak & Walk 2002, *A Distribution-Free Theory of Nonparametric Regression*).** Consistency for cubic partitions of side h_n requires h_n → 0 and n·h_n^d → ∞ (Thms 4.2, 23.1). The rate bound has the form E‖m̂−m‖² ≤ c·(σ² + sup|m|²)/(n·h_n^d) + d·C²·h_n², minimized at h_n ∝ n^(-1/(d+2)). [verified: consistency condition from Györfi & Walk; the rate as reproduced in a paper applying it] — [Györfi book PDF](https://www.szit.bme.hu/~gyorfi/bookestpre.pdf); [Lund WP18_12](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/194854013/WP18_12.pdf); [arXiv 1810.13259 (applies the bound)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.13259)
  - The consistency condition is the direct analogue: "regions must shrink (h → 0), but slowly enough that each still contains many samples (n·h^d → ∞)". In our setting, n is the number of training electrodes and each region needs many electrodes.

### Inferences
- **Mapping to embeddings.** A lookup-table region embedding is a piecewise-constant (regressogram) function of location, so the partitioning-estimate bias-variance analysis applies directly. The variance term per region scales as 1/(electrodes per region), and the bias term scales with within-region heterogeneity. The theory predicts that the optimal number of regions grows sublinearly with the number of electrodes, roughly K* ∝ n^(d/(d+2)), or about n^0.6 for d=3 under Lipschitz smoothness. Smoother location effects give slower growth.
- Two non-classical extras in the iEEG setting are not covered by histogram theory. Both push the optimum coarser and should be stated as additions:
  1. Electrode sampling is non-uniform and clustered by subject (effective n is closer to the number of subjects than the number of electrodes).
  2. Test subjects can land in regions with no training data. This is a "coverage" term with no analogue in the i.i.d. histogram setting, and it resembles the SDM transferability point.
- Continuous coordinates with Fourier features play the role of kernel bandwidth (bandwidth ∝ n^(-1/(d+4)) for kernel regression with twice-differentiable functions; standard theory, [impression] on the exact citation, e.g. Wasserman, *All of Nonparametric Statistics*). The analogous prediction is that the optimal maximum frequency should grow with N.
- **Novelty assessment.** The theory for why the optimum should shift finer with N is textbook and can be cited. Empirical demonstrations in neuroimaging are near-misses: Dohmatob 2021 shows an optimum whose benefit shrinks with n, and Thirion 2014 shows a fine-vs-coarse split by criterion. In ecology, Guisan 2007 shows larger samples are more grain-sensitive. Explicitly tracing the optimal-resolution curve against N for learned location embeddings in a neural foundation model appears unclaimed, based on this search.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the exact theorem number in Györfi et al. (2002) for the n^(-1/(d+2)) rate, nor a canonical textbook citation for the kernel-bandwidth rate n^(-1/(d+4)). Both should be checked in the books before citing.
- I found no prior derivation for the "unseen region at test time" term. It may need to be formulated as new.
